# Did the Latin Debt Crisis End Volcker's High Rates?
## And by analogy — what would actually make Warsh slow down?
### September 17, 2026 — Macro / Policy-Regime Analysis

> **The user's ask:** *"If the Russia-Ukraine war is the 1st oil crisis, the US-Iran war is the 2nd oil crisis, and Warsh's hike is the 1979 Volcker moment — then was it the Latin American debt crisis that ended Volcker's high-rate policy? Under that analogy, what would make Warsh slow down?"*

> **TL;DR — the premise is wrong in a way that changes the answer.**
> - **No. The Latin debt crisis did NOT end Volcker's tightening.** The Fed had **already cut twice before Mexico**: the Board voted cut #1 on **July 19, 1982** (effective Jul 20) and cut #2 on **July 30** — **24 and 13 days before** Finance Minister Silva Herzog's **August 12, 1982** call. The effective fed funds rate had already fallen **−1.56pp in July (14.15% → 12.59%) before Mexico said a word.**
> - **Three facts kill the thesis outright.** (1) Two of the seven 1982 cuts pre-date Mexico. (2) On **September 13, 1982 — at the height of the crisis — the Board REFUSED a further cut.** (3) The Fed then **re-tightened +3.13pp** (8.51% Feb-1983 → **11.64% Aug-1984**) *while the debt crisis was getting worse* — Continental Illinois failed in May 1984. At the **Brady Plan (Mar-1989)**, when the debt was finally reduced, fed funds was **9.85% — higher than the 1983 trough.** Rates bottomed **~6.5 years before** the crisis was resolved.
> - **What actually ended it: disinflation had been delivered and paid for.** CPI fell from **14.8% (Mar-1980) to 6.6% at the pivot to 3.8% by Dec-1982**, bought with the deepest post-war recession (**unemployment 10.8%**). The Fed's own history says it plainly: *"By October 1982, inflation had fallen to 5 percent… The Fed allowed the federal funds rate to fall back to 9 percent."* **Goodfriend & King's definitive account of the disinflation does not mention Mexico once.**
> - **What Mexico DID do:** it was an **accelerant and a lock-in**, not a trigger — and the Fed answered it with **liquidity tools, not rate cuts** (a $325m Fed swap inside a **$1.85bn BIS facility**, plus regulatory forbearance). **In private the FOMC admitted the fragility motive** — Vice Chairman Solomon, Aug 24, 1982: *"our putting monetary policy on the back burner because of our concern about fragility. And even though we are doing that, in a certain sense we are not."*
> - **The modern control experiment confirms it: SVB failed Mar 10, 2023 — and the Fed HIKED 25bp on Mar 22 while launching the BTFP.** EFFR went **4.57% → 5.12% (+0.55pp) straight through the banking crisis.** A financial accident gets a liquidity tool, not a pivot.
> - **So for Warsh, the debt-crisis channel is the WRONG tripwire.** The real preconditions are measurable, and **almost none are met**. At Volcker's pivot the **real policy rate was +6.0pp**; after the Sep-16-2026 hike Warsh's is **+0.2pp (headline) / +1.1pp (core)**. Unemployment was **9.8%** then, **4.1%** now. **Warsh is at the 1979 starting line, not the 1982 finish line — there is nothing yet to "un-do."**
> - **The single most likely thing that slows Warsh is not a debt crisis — it's Hormuz reopening.** Today's inflation impulse *is* the war: Brent **~$104–108**, Hormuz throughput **~2 mb/d vs 18–20 normal**, headline CPI **3.7%** vs core **2.8%**. **The whole gap between headline and core is the oil shock.** Remove it and the case for further hikes largely dissolves — *without any recession or default.*
> - **⚠️ Cross-report link (matters for positioning):** that same event is the **VLCC super-spike**. [TD3C at ~$1.035M/day](../vlcc_cycles/report_en) and Brent at $107 are **one event, two trades.** A Hormuz normalisation that stops Warsh **also collapses the tanker rate.** Do not own "Fed pivot" and "tanker spike" as if they were independent bets — **they are the same bet, in opposite directions.**
> - **The 1980 false dawn is the reason Warsh will be slow.** Volcker cut **−8.58pp in three months** (17.61% Apr-1980 → 9.03% Jul-1980) at a **−4.12pp real rate**, and was forced to **re-hike +9.87pp**. A chairman appointed explicitly for inflation credibility fears that precedent above all else.

## §1 — Method

The question is a **cyclical-regime** question, so the repo's **CRule 1 two-cycle backtrack** applies, with the tightening *regime* as the cycle:

- **Reference cycle:** Volcker, Oct-1979 → the 1982 pivot → the 1983-84 re-tightening.
- **Control cycle:** Powell, Mar-2022 → the 2023 SVB shock (a clean natural experiment on "does a financial accident stop a hiking cycle?").
- **Current cycle:** Warsh, Sep-2026 →  ?

The method is deliberately **inverted** relative to how the question was asked. Rather than ask *"was it the debt crisis?"* and hunt for confirming evidence, I:

1. **Establish the dated sequence first** (§4) — because a cause cannot postdate its effect;
2. **Look for disconfirming evidence** (§5) — what did the Fed do when the crisis was *worst*?
3. **Only then** assign causes, and separate **documented fact** from **mainstream interpretation** from **speculation**;
4. **Convert the true causes into measurable tripwires** (§8) and read today's values off the same instruments.

## §2 — Step 1: Concise Research Draft

### Core conclusion

**The Latin American debt crisis did not end Volcker's high-rate policy; disinflation did, and the debt crisis merely accelerated an easing already under way and then constrained the Fed from reversing it. By analogy, Warsh will not be slowed by an emerging-market or credit blow-up per se — he will be slowed when the inflation impulse itself fades, which today means primarily when the Hormuz oil shock passes, or when the labour market cracks. Warsh is at the 1979 starting line, not the 1982 finish line: his real policy rate is ~0%, so there is nothing yet to un-do.**

### Supporting point 1

**Claim → the Fed's easing chronologically PRE-DATES the Mexican default, so the default cannot be its cause.**
*Evidence needed:* the exact Board vote dates of every 1982 discount-rate change, from a primary Fed source; and the month-by-month effective fed funds rate for 1982. **Obtained — see §4.** Cut #1 voted **Jul 19**, cut #2 voted **Jul 30**, Mexico **Aug 12**. EFFR fell **−1.56pp in July, pre-Mexico.**

### Supporting point 2

**Claim → the Fed behaved *counter* to the debt-crisis thesis at the moments when the thesis predicts maximum easing.**
*Evidence needed:* Fed actions during the crisis peak, and during 1983–84 when the crisis spread. **Obtained — see §5.** The Board **refused** a cut on **Sep 13, 1982**, and **re-tightened +3.13pp into 1984**, when Brazil was being rescued and Continental Illinois failed.

### Supporting point 3

**Claim → the Fed's own stated reasons, and the academic consensus, attribute the pivot to delivered disinflation plus recession — not to foreign debt.**
*Evidence needed:* the stated rationale attached to each discount-rate vote; the Fed's official history; the leading academic account. **Obtained — see §4.3 and §6.** Every 1982 vote cites *market rates* and *restrained money and credit growth*. Goodfriend & King (NBER WP 11562) attribute the October 1982 shift to inflation at ~5% and **do not mention Mexico at all**.

### Opposing point 1 (against my own conclusion)

**Claim → the FOMC privately WAS easing for financial-fragility reasons, so the distinction between "disinflation" and "crisis" is partly artificial.**
*Evidence needed:* FOMC transcripts around the pivot. **Obtained, and it genuinely damages the clean version of my thesis — see §7.** Solomon (Aug 24, 1982): *"our putting monetary policy on the back burner because of our concern about fragility."* Volcker (Sep 24): *"the weaknesses in the financial system remain very evident both internationally and domestically."* Volcker personally instructed that the October directive mention *"the problems of foreign lending in particular… just to indicate that we are someplace in the real world."*

### Opposing point 2 (against my own conclusion)

**Claim → Mexico was a live Fed agenda item BEFORE August 12, so "pre-dates the default" may be measuring the wrong date.**
*Evidence needed:* FOMC discussion of Mexico before August. **Obtained, and it is a real weakness — see §7.** At the **Jun 30–Jul 1, 1982** FOMC, Volcker briefed the Committee on Mexico (*"it's all symptomatic of the international financial markets closing up pretty tightly on Mexico now"*), and the Fed had an existing **$700m swap** with Banco de México. The *stress* was visible ~6 weeks before the *announcement*. My timing argument refutes "**the August 12 default** caused the pivot"; it does **not** fully refute "**international fragility** contributed to it."

### Explicitly marked **unknown**

- Whether Warsh has a private financial-stability threshold — **unknown**; no transcript exists (2026 transcripts are released with a 5-year lag).
- Whether the Hormuz disruption persists — **unknown and unforecastable**; institutional Brent year-end forecasts span **$85 (Goldman) to $140+ (Rystad)**, which is an admission that nobody knows.

### 2. Logical leaps / equivocation (concept substitution)

- **The draft's biggest vulnerability is substituting "the August 12 default" for "the Latin American debt crisis."** These are different objects. The *default* is a dated event; the *crisis* is a process that was visible to the FOMC by **June 30, 1982** and arguably from 1981. The chronology refutes the first, not the second. **§2's own Opposing Point 2 concedes this, which is correct, but the Core Conclusion is still phrased more strongly than the evidence supports.** The defensible claim is: *the Latin debt crisis was neither necessary nor sufficient for the 1982 pivot* — supported by §5 — rather than *"it played no causal role."*
- **"Disinflation did it" is itself a substitution.** Disinflation is not an action; the FOMC's *belief* that disinflation was durable is. The operative variable is **credibility**, which is unobservable. The draft should not present "inflation fell to 5%" as a mechanism without noting it works only via expectations.
- **The Volcker↔Warsh mapping equivocates on "Volcker moment."** Volcker's October 1979 act was a **regime change** — abandoning a fed-funds target for nonborrowed-reserve targeting, deliberately surrendering control of the rate. **Warsh has raised rates 25bp and kept the existing framework.** Calling it "the Volcker moment" imports connotations the policy does not carry. The draft uses the user's framing without auditing it. **§4.1 must do so.**
- **The oil-crisis mapping is also loose.** 1973 was an *embargo* (politically withheld supply); 1979 was a *revolution* (lost production); 2026 is a *chokepoint blockade* (transit denial, with production intact). The third is the most reversible of the three — which cuts directly against a "this is the 1970s again" reading.

### 3. Missing counterexamples / competing explanations

- **The strongest competing explanation is under-weighted: the domestic financial accidents.** Drysdale (May) and **Penn Square (July 5)** precede the July pivot far more tightly than Mexico does. At the **July 15 conference call** Volcker said the Desk had been *"more alert… to keep a little ahead of the market"* and noted *"an undertone of nervousness."* If the draft says fragility mattered, **the fragility that mattered in July was American, not Mexican.** This is *more* damaging to the "Mexico did it" thesis, not less — but the draft under-uses it.
- **A missing counterexample the draft should have supplied: 1980.** Volcker *did* pivot for non-inflation reasons once (the credit-control recession) and it failed. That is the best available evidence on what a Fed chair does under pressure — and it argues Warsh eases *late*. **Added as §6.**
- **A missing modern control: March 2023.** The draft asserts "financial accident ≠ pivot" from 1982 alone, n=1. **SVB gives a second observation, and it agrees.** **Added as §5.3.**
- **Competing explanation not addressed: political pressure.** 2026 has open presidential pressure for cuts. In 1982 the evidence for political capitulation is weak, but the draft dismisses the modern analogue too quickly. It should be listed as a live channel with **low confidence**, since Fed independence is exactly what is being tested.
- **Missing: the fiscal/debt-service channel.** In 1982 US federal debt was ~30% of GDP; today it is far higher, so the *government's own* interest burden is a pressure on policy that had no 1982 analogue. **Not quantified here — flagged as a gap.**

## §4 — The analogy, audited; and the dated sequence

### §4.1 Does the user's mapping hold? A three-part audit

| Element of the analogy | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Russia-Ukraine ≈ 1st oil crisis (1973)** | **Partly.** Both are politically-driven supply disruptions with a re-routing outcome. But 1973 was a *deliberate embargo* against specific countries; 2022 was *sanctions-driven re-routing* in which barrels largely kept flowing to different buyers. **Global supply loss in 2022 was far smaller.** | Crude kept flowing to India/China; the tanker market lengthened ton-miles rather than losing cargo |
| **US-Iran ≈ 2nd oil crisis (1979)** | **Closest of the three, and getting closer.** 1979 = lost Iranian production; 2026 = a **chokepoint blockade**. The IEA calls it *"the largest supply shock in the history of the oil market,"* with ~**3.5 mb/d** of losses since March and **Hormuz throughput ~2 mb/d vs 18–20 normal**. | Brent **~$104–108** (Sep 14-16, 2026) |
| **Warsh ≈ Volcker moment (Oct 1979)** | **Rhetorically yes; substantively NOT YET.** Volcker changed the **operating regime** (nonborrowed-reserve targeting, surrendering rate control), which let the funds rate run to **22.36%**. Warsh has delivered **one 25bp hike to 3.75–4.00%** inside the existing framework — a **+0.2pp real rate**. | §8 |

**The audit's most important finding:** the first two mappings are about **supply shocks**, and they broadly hold. The third is about a **policy regime**, and it does not — yet. **That asymmetry is the answer to the question.** The oil-shock side of the analogy is real, which is why inflation is a live problem; the Volcker side is aspirational, which is why **the "what ends it" question is premature by construction.** You cannot ask what ends a Volcker regime that has not begun.

**One critical disanalogy on inflation itself.** Volcker inherited **a decade of unanchored expectations** — CPI was already **11% in June 1979** *before* the second oil shock landed. Warsh inherits **core CPI of 2.76%**. The same barrel price produces far less second-round inflation when expectations are anchored. **Concretely: today's entire headline-core gap (3.71% − 2.76% = 0.95pp) is the energy shock.** That is why Warsh needs 4%, not 19%.

### §4.4 The technical cover — what Volcker actually said on October 9, 1982

> *"We face over the next few months not just the possibility but the virtual certainty of distortions… in the M1 number… Right now, and over the next few weeks, some **$31 billion of 'all savers certificates' are maturing**… **We know M1 will be affected, but we simply have no way of measuring the degree of that shifting.**… In the circumstances, I do not believe that, in actual implementation of monetary policy, we have any alternative but to attach much less than usual weight to movements in M1 over the period immediately ahead."*
> — **Federal Reserve Bulletin, Nov 1982, pp. 691–92**

And, in the same speech, the explicit denial that anything had changed:

> *"the small reduction in the discount rate — as in the case of the **four changes of similar magnitude in July and August** — **represents no change in the basic thrust of policy**… Lower interest rates in an economy in recession are not unusual and are consistent with the need for recovery."*

**Read that carefully: Volcker himself grouped the post-Mexico cuts with the pre-Mexico ones as a single, unchanged policy.** That is the strongest possible contemporaneous statement against the "Mexico changed the policy" thesis — from the man who would have known.

## §5 — The decisive refutation: the Fed re-tightened INTO the crisis

This is the section that settles it. If the Latin debt crisis had ended the high-rate policy, rates should have **stayed** down until the crisis was resolved. They did not.

| Date | EFFR | What was happening in the debt crisis |
|---|---|---|
| **Feb 1983** | **8.51%** | **Cycle trough.** Mexico's IMF programme unsettled; Brazil rescue under way ($4.5bn+ of new bank money, BIS bridge) |
| Aug 1983 | **9.56%** | Crisis **spreading** — by Oct-1983, **27 countries owing $239bn** had rescheduled or were doing so |
| **May 1984** | **10.32%** | **Continental Illinois fails** — the largest US bank failure to that date |
| **Aug 1984** | **11.64%** | **Peak of the re-tightening: +3.13pp above the trough**, with the LDC crisis at its worst |
| Oct 1985 | 7.99% | **Baker Plan** — reschedules, does **not** reduce, the debt |
| Mar 1989 | **9.85%** | **Brady Plan** — actual principal forgiveness at last (~$61bn, ~⅓ of the debt, 18 countries, 1989–94) |

**Three observations that are individually damaging and jointly fatal to the thesis:**

1. **The Fed raised rates by +3.13pp while the crisis was worsening.** A crisis that "ended" the tightening evidently did not prevent a new one.
2. **When the debt was finally *reduced* (Brady, Mar-1989), fed funds was 9.85% — HIGHER than the 8.51% trough six years earlier.** The rate cycle and the debt cycle are **decoupled**.
3. **Citibank did not even provision for the losses until 1987** — five years after the "crisis that ended the tightening."

### §5.1 What the Fed actually used on Mexico

Not a rate cut. **A $325m Fed swap inside a $1.85bn BIS-aegis facility (Treasury $600m), announced Aug 30, 1982**, plus **regulatory forbearance** — letting banks delay recognising losses. (FRB Bulletin Sep 1982; FDIC ch. 5.)

**This is the structural lesson, and it is the single most transferable finding in this report:**

> **A central bank answers a solvency/funding crisis with LIQUIDITY and REGULATORY tools, and reserves the policy rate for inflation. The two are deliberately separated.**

### §5.2 The 1982 "separation principle," restated

| Problem | 1982 tool |
|---|---|
| Inflation at 6.6% and falling | The **discount rate / funds rate** |
| Mexico cannot pay $80bn | **Swap lines + BIS bridge + IMF programme** |
| US banks 176–290% of capital exposed | **Regulatory forbearance** (delayed loss recognition) |
| Penn Square depositors | **FDIC payoff** |

### §5.3 The modern control experiment — SVB, March 2023 (n=2)

The 1982 case is a single observation. **2023 provides a second, and it agrees.**

| Month | EFFR | CPI YoY | Event |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feb 2023 | 4.57% | 5.96% | |
| **Mar 2023** | **4.65%** | 4.92% | **SVB fails Mar 10. The Fed HIKES 25bp on Mar 22 and launches the BTFP the same week.** |
| Apr 2023 | 4.83% | 4.95% | |
| Jul 2023 | **5.12%** | 3.29% | |

**EFFR went 4.57% → 5.12% (+0.55pp) straight through the banking crisis.** The liquidity facility (BTFP) and the policy rate moved in **opposite directions on the same day** — the 1982 separation principle, executed in public, in the modern era.

> **Therefore: "a debt/credit crisis will force Warsh to stop" is refuted twice — in 1982 and in 2023.** What a crisis buys you is a **facility**, not a **cut**.

## §6 — The 1980 false dawn: why the second pivot needed a +6pp real rate

The user's question assumes the tightening needed an *external* event to end it. The history says the opposite: **the binding constraint was internal — Volcker had already failed once.**

| | Apr 1980 | **Jul 1980** | Dec 1980 | Jun 1981 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EFFR | 17.61% | **9.03%** | 18.90% | **19.10%** |
| CPI YoY | 14.59% | 13.15% | 12.35% | 9.70% |
| Unemployment | 6.9% | 7.8% | 7.2% | 7.5% |
| **Real policy rate** | **+3.0pp** | **−4.12pp** | **+6.6pp** | **+9.40pp** |

**Volcker cut −8.58pp in three months, at a NEGATIVE −4.12pp real rate — and was forced to re-hike +9.87pp.** The Fed's own history calls this out:

> *"Volcker's first attempt to lower inflation and inflationary expectations proved insufficient… As unemployment mounted, the Fed eased up, **an action reminiscent of the 'stop-go' policies the public had come to expect**."*
> — Federal Reserve History, "Recession of 1981–82"

And the market punished it: **the 10-year yield rose from ~11% (Oct-1980) to >15% a year later** *"possibly because the market believed the Fed would back down"* (Goodfriend & King 2005). **The premature pivot raised long rates.**

**This is the most important lesson for 2026.** The reason the 1982 pivot came at **+6.0pp real** rather than at −4pp is that **the −4pp pivot had already been tried and had failed publicly.** Warsh — appointed explicitly on inflation credibility, facing open political pressure to cut — is in precisely the position where that precedent binds hardest.

> **Prediction that follows (and is labelled speculation): Warsh's error will be to stop too LATE, not too early.** A chair whose entire mandate is credibility does not risk the 1980 replay.

## §7 — The honest counter-case: what the transcripts show

**Peer Review §2 was right that my Core Conclusion was over-stated.** Here is the evidence *against* my own verdict, presented in full.

### 7.1 The FOMC was privately easing for fragility reasons

**Vice Chairman Anthony Solomon (President, FRBNY), August 24, 1982** — the single most damaging quote to my thesis:

> *"We will get more stories, anyway, about our **putting monetary policy on the back burner because of our concern about fragility. And even though we are doing that**, in a certain sense we are not; we are luckily enough consistent with our monetary policy [targets] at the moment."*

**Volcker, same meeting:**

> *"I, frankly, cannot live in these circumstances, given what is going on in the money markets, with violent moves in short-term rates in either direction… it's just the wrong policy, period, during this particular period."*

**Volcker, September 24, 1982 conference call:**

> *"The problems of Mexico have in no way cleared up; the problems of the rest of Latin America are clearly being compounded… **The weaknesses in the financial system remain very evident both internationally and domestically**… they are **not very far back from being on the edge of their chairs**."*

**Governor Lyle Gramley, October 5, 1982:**

> *"I think **the world economy is literally starved for liquidity**… Diets are very good things unless they get out of hand… the patient becomes **anorexic**… I am worried that we have gone on long enough starving the world economy for liquidity."*

### 7.2 Volcker personally put Mexico into the published directive

At the October 5, 1982 meeting, **Volcker instructed the staff to insert the reference**:

> **CHAIRMAN VOLCKER:** *"I think this could use a judicious sentence or two, making some allusion to the strains or pressures… in the banking sector these days and **to the problems of foreign lending in particular** — it needs to be expressed very judiciously — just to indicate that **we are someplace in the real world**… something expressed very calmly, but **not ignored entirely**."*

The resulting sentence, **Oct 5, 1982 Domestic Policy Directive** (FRB Bulletin, Dec 1982, p. 765):

> *"…reflecting some well-publicized problems in recent months of a few banks here and abroad and **the financing difficulties of Mexico**, a more cautious atmosphere in private credit markets has been reflected in wider spreads…"*

**This is the only published Fed reference to Mexico in the entire easing sequence — and it is framed as market *background*, not as a policy *reason*.** But Volcker put it there deliberately, which means it mattered to him.

### 7.3 Mexico was on the agenda six weeks BEFORE August 12

At the **June 30–July 1, 1982** FOMC, Volcker briefed the Committee on Mexico and the Fed provided swap "window-dressing money" ahead of the July 4 Mexican election. **A $700m swap with Banco de México was already outstanding.**

### 7.4 Weighing it — the revised verdict

| Candidate cause | Documentary support | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Delivered disinflation + deep recession** | **Strongest.** All seven stated vote rationales; Fed History; Goodfriend & King (**zero mentions of Mexico**) | **Primary cause** |
| **Domestic financial fragility** (Drysdale, Penn Square) | **Strong, and chronologically the tightest fit to the July pivot** | **Genuine contributing cause** |
| **M1 distortion** | **Documented as the stated reason** (Oct 9 speech) — but transcripts show the Committee knew it doubled as cover | **Proximate mechanism + cover** |
| **Latin debt crisis** | Transcripts show it weighed on the Committee — **but it postdates two cuts, the Board refused a cut mid-crisis, and the Fed re-tightened into it** | **Accelerant and lock-in. Neither necessary nor sufficient.** |
| Political pressure | Volcker framed as resisting Congress; no transcript evidence of capitulation | **Weak** |

> **Revised Core Conclusion (narrowed per Peer Review §2):** *The Latin American debt crisis was **neither necessary nor sufficient** to end Volcker's high-rate policy. It accelerated an easing already under way, supplied a financial-stability rationale, and made reversal harder — but the tightening ended because the **disinflation had been delivered and paid for**, and the Fed proved this by **re-tightening +3.13pp into the worst of the crisis**.*

## §9 — Who is today's "Mexico"? The modern fragility map

The user's instinct — *find the thing that breaks* — is a good one; it is just aimed at the wrong tripwire. Here is where the modern equivalents sit.

| 1982 fragility | 2026 analogue | Reading today | Stressed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nine money-center banks at 176–290% of capital in LDC debt** | US/EU bank EM exposure | European bank EM exposure reported ~**$340bn** *(secondary, unverified)* — **nothing remotely like 2.9× capital** | ❌ **No** |
| **Mexico: $80bn, cannot pay** | EM sovereign refinancing | **~$1.4tn** of EM bonds to refinance late-2026→early-2027, much above 6.5% yields; IMF expects **8–12** restructurings; **40% of low-income countries** at debt-distress risk *(secondary — F6)* | ⚠️ **Building, not breaking** |
| **Credit-market stress** | US high-yield OAS | **2.76%** — **19th percentile** of the available window | ❌ **No — tight** |
| **International lending stress** | **EM corporate OAS** | **1.37%** — **1st percentile; the window LOW was set on Sep 10, 2026** | ❌ **No — at its tightest** |
| **Drysdale / Penn Square** (non-bank leverage) | Private credit, NBFI leverage | **No public accident identified. Opacity is the risk; there is no spread to read.** | ❓ **Unknown** |
| Continental Illinois | A systemically-important failure | None | ❌ **No** |

**⚠️ F3 applies:** those percentiles are within a **~3-year** window only. But the **levels** are unambiguous: **at the exact moment the user asks "where is the debt crisis?", EM corporate credit is at its tightest of the whole available window — set one week ago.**

**The honest summary:** the modern EM stress is a **slow-burn refinancing problem**, not a **sudden solvency shock into an over-levered banking system**. And per §5, even the 1982 version — which *was* existential — produced **swap lines and forbearance, not rate cuts.**

> **The blind spot to name explicitly:** the one box marked ❓ is **private credit / NBFI leverage**, which by construction has **no observable spread**. 1982's surprise (Drysdale, Penn Square) came from exactly that kind of unobserved leverage. **If a 2026 accident happens, it most likely comes from there — and per §5.3 it would still get a facility, not a cut.**

### §10.2 The ranked answer

**1. Hormuz reopens — BY FAR the most likely and the fastest (T1).**
Today's inflation problem *is* the war. Brent **~$104–108**; Hormuz throughput **~2 mb/d vs 18–20 normal**; ~**3.5 mb/d** of losses since March (IEA). **Core is already 2.76%.** If transit normalises, headline converges down toward core within roughly two quarters and **the case for further hikes largely dissolves — with no recession and no default required.** This is the *only* path that stops Warsh **without economic damage**, which is exactly why it is the most likely.

**2. The labour market cracks (T3).** The classic path, and the one that actually ended 1982. Watch the **Sahm rule (+0.30 today vs +0.50 trigger vs +2.23 in Jul-1982)**. Slower, and it requires real damage.

**3. Warsh reaches a restrictive real rate and simply stops (T2).** Note the distinction: **"stops hiking" ≠ "cuts."** With core at 2.76%, a ~2pp real rate implies a nominal policy rate around **4.75–5.00%** — i.e. **roughly 4 more 25bp hikes**. Reaching it is *itself* a form of slowing.

**4. A financial accident (T4) — the user's hypothesis. Ranked LAST on rate impact, and it is not close.** Refuted twice (1982: refused a cut on Sep 13 and re-tightened +3.13pp; 2023: hiked 25bp eleven days after SVB). **Expect a facility, not a pivot.**

**5. Political capitulation — low confidence, non-zero.** Open presidential pressure exists; Warsh's answer has been *"We stay in our lane."* Unmeasurable, and the thing his appointment was supposed to preclude.
